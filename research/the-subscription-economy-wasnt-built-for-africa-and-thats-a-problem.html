The Subscription Economy Wasn't Built for Africa. And That's a Problem.

Why African technology founders need to rethink how they monetise digital products.

I have been thinking about a question that I believe more African technology founders need to confront: Can you really build a sustainable technology business in Africa by relying on monthly subscriptions?

It sounds like a straightforward question. The subscription model has become almost synonymous with the technology industry. From software and cloud computing to streaming platforms and artificial intelligence, companies have built enormous businesses around one simple proposition: pay a fixed amount every month to access a product.

Investors like it. Founders like it. Financial models look attractive with predictable monthly recurring revenue, relatively straightforward customer lifetime value calculations and the possibility of compounding revenue as the customer base grows.

But I think we have become too comfortable treating this model as universally applicable.

Building technology for a market and building a business around that market are two different challenges. And somewhere between acquiring the customer and convincing them to pay every thirty days, many African technology businesses encounter a problem that their original business models never anticipated.

The problem is not necessarily the product. It is the economics of the customer.

We are importing a business model without examining its assumptions

Most subscription businesses are designed around a particular understanding of consumer behaviour.

A customer has predictable income, has access to an established payment instrument, can accommodate recurring expenses and is willing to maintain a financial commitment for continued access to a service.

These assumptions are not unreasonable. They work in many markets.

But how well do they translate into African economies?

Consider the structure of employment. The IMF has highlighted the substantial scale of informal employment in Sub-Saharan Africa, estimated at approximately 70% of total employment.

That matters enormously to a technology company.

A freelancer might earn significantly in one month and very little in the next. A small business might experience considerable fluctuations in revenue. A young professional might have the financial capacity to subscribe to five digital products today but reconsider those expenses when other priorities arise.

These are not necessarily customers who lack purchasing power. They are customers whose purchasing power can be inconsistent.

And there is a fundamental difference between having the money to purchase a product today and being able to commit to purchasing it every month.

The conventional subscription model is built around the second assumption.

I think African technology companies need to examine that assumption much more closely.

The customer is not necessarily rejecting your product

There is something particularly interesting about the relationship between perceived product value and willingness to pay.

A customer can find an AI assistant incredibly useful, use it to research, write, organise work and solve problems, and still cancel their subscription.

That might appear contradictory to a founder.

It isn't.

The customer is making a financial decision, not necessarily a judgement about your technology.

For many people, digital subscriptions compete with expenses that cannot easily be postponed. Housing, transport, food, education, electricity and connectivity take priority.

When disposable income becomes constrained, a digital subscription is one of the expenses that can be reconsidered.

This creates a difficult situation for companies building products that customers genuinely appreciate but do not necessarily consider essential enough to maintain throughout the year.

We need to stop assuming that product usefulness automatically translates into subscription retention.

Sometimes the customer wants the product but does not want the financial commitment attached to it.

That distinction should influence how we design our businesses.

AI makes the subscription problem even more interesting

This is where I believe the conversation becomes particularly important for African AI startups.

Unlike some traditional digital products, AI has a direct and sometimes substantial cost of consumption.

Every interaction can involve model inference, computing resources, storage and other infrastructure expenses. The cost of serving one customer can be considerably different from the cost of serving another.

A customer who uses an AI platform occasionally and another who uses it extensively are not necessarily generating the same infrastructure costs.

Yet conventional subscription models often place them within the same pricing structure.

This creates an interesting economic imbalance.

The company promises a fixed amount of access, but its underlying cost of delivering that access can fluctuate considerably.

Now introduce the African market.

You want your product to be affordable. You want to attract a large user base. You want to compete with established international AI companies. You also need to pay for the technology that makes your product possible.

How do you balance these competing requirements?

Reducing prices is not always the answer. If the cost of serving a customer exceeds the revenue generated by that customer, acquiring more users can actually make the business less sustainable.

And pricing a product beyond the customer's purchasing capacity creates a different problem: you might have an excellent product with very little commercial adoption.

This is an economic problem, not simply a marketing problem.

The challenge for AI founders is to build pricing structures that reflect both the customer's ability to pay and the actual cost of delivering intelligence.

We have mistaken digital payments for subscription readiness

Africa's mobile money revolution is one of the continent's most important technological achievements.

Kenya, for example, has demonstrated that people can adopt digital financial services at extraordinary scale.

But I think we need to distinguish between the ability to make digital payments and the willingness and ability to maintain recurring financial commitments.

These are not the same things.

Someone can comfortably make a mobile money transaction whenever they need to. That does not necessarily mean they want a platform automatically deducting money from their account every month.

There is a psychological difference, a payment infrastructure difference and, in some cases, a trust difference.

Many digital products still approach African customers with payment arrangements designed primarily around international debit cards and conventional recurring billing.

But the customer may be more comfortable with a prepaid wallet, a deliberate transaction or a payment made when a particular service is needed.

We should not underestimate how much friction this creates.

The success of mobile money should encourage technology companies to rethink payment experiences rather than assume that existing payment infrastructure has solved every monetisation challenge.

Connectivity is another cost that founders overlook

There is a tendency to discuss the affordability of digital products by looking exclusively at their subscription prices.

I think that is incomplete.

A customer using an AI product is already paying for the internet connection required to access it. They may also be paying for electricity, a device and other services necessary to use the platform.

The GSMA's State of Mobile Internet Connectivity Report 2024 highlighted the significant affordability barriers facing mobile internet users in Sub-Saharan Africa. Its 2023 data showed that 20GB of mobile data represented approximately 15% of average monthly income in the region.

That means the actual cost of accessing a digital product extends beyond the amount displayed on its pricing page.

For AI companies, this is especially relevant.

A product that requires continuous connectivity, large document uploads or intensive multimedia interactions may impose additional costs on the customer.

We should therefore be asking a broader question: how do we make technology affordable to use, not simply affordable to purchase?

That requires efficient infrastructure, thoughtful product design and an understanding of how people actually access digital services.

The obsession with monthly recurring revenue

I also think the African startup ecosystem needs to have a more honest conversation about what constitutes a healthy technology business.

We have become accustomed to celebrating user registrations, monthly recurring revenue and subscription growth.

These are useful metrics, but they do not tell the entire story.

A company can have thousands of paying customers and still struggle financially if the cost of acquiring and serving those customers is too high.

An AI startup can grow its user base while simultaneously increasing its infrastructure obligations.

A subscription business can report impressive revenue one month and experience substantial cancellations the next.

The important question is not simply how many people subscribed.

It is whether the company has developed a commercially sustainable relationship with its customers.

Can it retain them? Can it serve them profitably? Can it afford to continue improving its product? Can it survive when customers reduce their spending?

For founders, these questions are considerably more important than the appearance of growth.

Recurring revenue is valuable, but recurring costs are equally real.

Perhaps we should stop treating every customer as a monthly subscriber

I am increasingly convinced that African technology companies need to experiment with more flexible monetisation structures.

I don't believe subscriptions should disappear. There are products that deliver continuous value and justify recurring payments. Enterprise software, cloud infrastructure, accounting systems and business-critical platforms are obvious examples.

But there are other products where a rigid monthly subscription might not be the most appropriate starting point.

Consider an AI platform.

Why should every customer be forced into the same financial arrangement?

Some customers might prefer a monthly subscription. Others might want to purchase credits and use them whenever necessary. Some might need access for a few days to complete a particular project. Businesses might prefer an organisational account with centralised billing.

These are different customer requirements, and there is no compelling reason to force all of them into one payment structure.

I see an opportunity in combining several models:

  • Subscriptions: For customers who use a product consistently and want predictable access.
  • Usage-based payments: For customers who prefer paying according to consumption.
  • Prepaid credits: For customers who want to control their spending without recurring commitments.
  • Short-term access: For customers whose needs are occasional or project-based.
  • Enterprise arrangements: For organisations that require predictable access for multiple users.

The objective should not be to eliminate recurring revenue. It should be to create multiple paths to revenue.

A company should not lose a customer simply because that customer cannot maintain a monthly subscription.

There should be another way for that customer to continue accessing the product and generating revenue for the company.

And this is why every startup needs a non-technical person in its ranks

There is another dimension to this conversation that I think deserves attention.

Technology startups are often built by people who understand technology exceptionally well. They can develop products, design systems, work with models, build infrastructure and solve complex engineering problems.

But building a technically impressive product and building a commercially sustainable company require different kinds of thinking.

This is where I believe every startup needs a non-technical person in its ranks.

Someone who can look beyond the code, the architecture and the product roadmap. Someone who constantly asks uncomfortable questions about the market, customer behaviour, pricing, distribution, revenue and the economics of keeping the company operational.

A technically brilliant founder might spend months improving a product without sufficiently examining whether the intended customer can afford it, how frequently they will use it or whether the company can make money serving them.

The non-technical perspective brings a different set of questions.

Who exactly are we building for? How does this customer earn money? What makes them return? What would make them stop paying? How do we reach them without spending more on acquisition than we can recover? And perhaps most importantly, how do we turn technological capability into a viable business?

These are not secondary questions. They are fundamental to the survival of a startup.

I am not suggesting that every startup needs another executive or that technical founders cannot understand business. Quite the opposite. The strongest founding teams are those that appreciate the importance of both disciplines.

Technology creates the capability. Commercial thinking helps determine whether that capability can become a sustainable business.

In markets like ours, where purchasing power, payment behaviour and market conditions can differ considerably from those assumed by imported business models, this distinction becomes even more important.

Sometimes the person who saves a startup is not the person who writes the best code. It is the person who recognises that the company is building something people cannot sustainably pay for, and has the courage to challenge the entire business model.

The opportunity is not to make technology cheaper at any cost

There is one point I particularly want to emphasise.

Building for Africa should not mean assuming that African customers should receive inferior technology or that companies must operate permanently on unsustainable margins.

That would be another mistake.

We want world-class AI systems, software infrastructure, research capabilities and digital products.

We want companies capable of competing internationally.

But achieving that requires business models that recognise the economic realities of the markets they serve.

An African technology company cannot simply copy the pricing page of an American SaaS company, convert the dollar amount into Kenyan shillings and assume it has developed a viable African pricing strategy.

Nor should it sacrifice its ability to invest in research, infrastructure and product development just to achieve an attractive entry price.

Affordability and sustainability must exist together.

That is the difficult part of building technology businesses in emerging markets.

The question African founders should be asking

I don't think the future of African technology should be defined by how successfully we reproduce business models developed elsewhere.

We should be asking whether those models actually make sense for the customers we are building for.

Mobile money demonstrated that technological innovation can emerge from understanding local economic realities rather than forcing existing systems onto new markets.

I believe digital products deserve the same thinking.

The next generation of African technology companies will need to be particularly deliberate about how they charge, how they collect payments, how they manage infrastructure costs and how they retain customers.

And perhaps the most important shift is this: we need to stop viewing the subscription as the default measure of a customer's commitment to a product.

A customer who purchases credits every week, pays for a service when they need it or uses a prepaid wallet may be just as valuable as someone who pays a monthly subscription.

The business model should recognise that.

My position is simple: Africa does not need fewer subscription products. It needs fewer technology companies assuming that a subscription is the only way to build a sustainable business.

We have spent considerable time discussing how to build technology for Africa.

It is time we spent just as much time discussing how to build viable businesses around that technology.
