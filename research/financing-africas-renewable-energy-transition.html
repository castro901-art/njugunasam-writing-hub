Financing Africa's Renewable Energy Transition

The role of public finance, private capital and development institutions

“Act so that the effects of your action are compatible with the permanence of genuine human life.”

— Hans Jonas, The Imperative of Responsibility (English translation, 1984), ch. 1/V

Africa's energy transition is not simply an environmental objective. It is an economic development priority.

Reliable and affordable electricity is essential to industrialisation, agricultural processing, digital services, healthcare, education and the growth of small businesses. Without sufficient energy infrastructure, African economies will struggle to expand production, attract investment and create the conditions for long-term economic transformation.

The continent has significant renewable energy resources, including solar, wind, geothermal and hydropower. Yet the existence of these resources does not automatically translate into electricity generation. Projects must be financed, constructed, connected to grids and supported by institutions capable of maintaining reliable energy systems.

The central challenge is therefore not only how to generate renewable electricity. It is how to finance the infrastructure required to make it economically accessible.

The investment gap

Africa's energy investment position reveals a considerable imbalance between its development needs and the capital reaching the sector.

The International Energy Agency's World Energy Investment 2025 report estimated that Africa accounts for approximately 2% of global clean-energy investment despite representing around 20% of the world's population.

The report also identifies major differences between regions within the continent. South Africa and North Africa attract a disproportionate share of energy investment, while many Sub-Saharan African countries continue to face limited access to reliable electricity.

This imbalance has consequences for economic development. Businesses operating without reliable power face higher operating costs, production interruptions and greater uncertainty. Households face barriers to accessing modern energy services, while governments must address the fiscal and social consequences of inadequate infrastructure.

Closing the investment gap requires a combination of public funding, private capital, development finance and improved project preparation.

Renewable energy is increasingly competitive

The economic case for renewable energy has strengthened as technology costs have declined.

According to the International Renewable Energy Agency's 2025 report on renewable power generation costs in 2024, 91% of newly commissioned utility-scale renewable capacity produced electricity at a lower cost than the cheapest new fossil-fuel alternative.

Solar photovoltaic technology has become particularly competitive in many markets. Wind, geothermal and hydropower also offer important opportunities depending on local resource conditions, grid requirements and project economics.

However, the cost of generating electricity is not the only consideration. A project must also account for transmission, distribution, storage, land, financing, construction and operational requirements.

A low-cost solar plant, for example, cannot deliver its full economic value if the electricity cannot be transmitted to consumers or if the grid cannot accommodate variable generation.

This is why renewable energy investment must be considered alongside investment in transmission networks, distribution systems, storage and regional electricity interconnections.

The cost of capital

One of the most important barriers to renewable energy investment in Africa is the cost of financing.

Renewable energy projects are capital-intensive. Much of the expenditure occurs before a project begins generating revenue, and investors recover their investment over many years.

The cost of capital therefore has a direct effect on the price of electricity.

IRENA's 2025 analysis illustrates this challenge. Its weighted average cost of capital assumptions for 2024 ranged from 3.8% in Europe to 12% in Africa. These are report assumptions reflecting prevailing macroeconomic conditions, not universal borrowing rates for every project or country.

The report also found that financing costs represented a particularly significant share of the levelised cost of onshore wind electricity in Africa.

This matters because two projects with similar equipment and construction costs can produce electricity at different prices when their financing conditions differ.

Investors consider currency risk, sovereign risk, payment security, political and regulatory uncertainty, interest rates and the financial position of electricity purchasers.

Reducing these risks can make otherwise viable projects more attractive without requiring governments to absorb every project cost.

The role of public finance

Public finance remains essential to the development of energy infrastructure.

Governments can fund transmission networks, rural electrification, energy planning, feasibility studies and other infrastructure that may not generate sufficient direct revenue to attract private investment.

They can also improve the investment environment through transparent procurement, predictable regulation, credible power-purchase arrangements and effective utility governance.

Public funding is particularly important where projects deliver broad economic and social benefits that cannot be captured by a private investor.

However, public resources are limited. Governments facing high debt-service obligations cannot finance every energy project through borrowing or budget allocations.

The challenge is to use public finance strategically: funding essential infrastructure, addressing market failures and reducing specific risks that prevent private investment.

Mobilising private capital

Private investors can provide substantial financing for renewable energy generation and related infrastructure.

This includes commercial banks, infrastructure funds, pension funds, insurance companies, institutional investors and specialised energy developers.

Yet private capital will not enter every market simply because renewable resources are available. Investors need projects that are properly prepared, financially viable and supported by credible contractual arrangements.

Project preparation is therefore critical.

Feasibility studies, environmental and social assessments, land arrangements, grid-connection plans, revenue forecasts and transparent procurement processes can improve the bankability of projects.

Blended finance can also help. Development finance institutions may provide concessional loans, guarantees, technical assistance or risk-sharing instruments to improve the financial structure of projects.

The objective should be to use limited public and concessional resources to mobilise additional private investment, rather than permanently subsidising projects that cannot become sustainable.

Regional power markets

Energy investment should not be viewed only through national boundaries.

Regional electricity trade can help countries balance differences in demand, generation capacity and renewable resources. Interconnected grids can support more efficient use of power infrastructure and create larger markets for electricity producers.

The Eastern Africa Power Pool and other regional initiatives demonstrate the importance of cross-border cooperation.

However, regional trade requires more than transmission lines. Countries need compatible technical standards, effective power-trading arrangements, reliable payment systems and institutions capable of managing cross-border electricity markets.

Regional integration can improve project economics, but it requires sustained political and institutional cooperation.

A stronger investment framework

Africa's renewable energy transition requires a financing framework that connects public policy with commercially viable investment.

Governments should prioritise grid infrastructure, improve the financial health of electricity utilities, strengthen procurement systems and develop credible long-term energy plans.

Development finance institutions should focus on reducing risks that prevent otherwise viable projects from attracting private capital. Local financial markets should also be developed so that projects can access longer-term financing in domestic currencies where possible.

Investors, meanwhile, need to assess projects on their actual economic and financial merits rather than relying on broad assumptions about African markets.

The energy transition will not be financed through public budgets alone, nor will private investment solve every infrastructure challenge.

The objective should be a balanced model in which public finance creates the conditions for investment, private capital supports commercially viable projects and development institutions help bridge gaps that markets cannot address independently.

Africa's renewable energy resources are an important economic opportunity. Turning that opportunity into reliable electricity, productive businesses and wider economic growth will depend on the quality of the financing structures built around them.
