Fiscal Space and Infrastructure Investment in Africa

Rethinking how the continent finances long-term economic development

Africa's infrastructure challenge is often described as a shortage of money. But the problem is more complicated than the amount of capital available.

It is also about how governments allocate public resources, how infrastructure projects are selected, how borrowing is managed and whether public investment produces enough economic value to justify its cost.

Roads, electricity networks, ports, railways, water systems and digital infrastructure are not simply government expenditure items. They are productive assets that influence the cost of doing business, the movement of goods, access to markets and the ability of economies to grow.

The challenge for African governments is to create enough fiscal space to invest in these assets without undermining debt sustainability or essential public services.

“Development can be seen, it is argued here, as a process of expanding the real freedoms that people enjoy.”

— Amartya Sen, Development as Freedom, 1999, p. 3

Understanding fiscal space

Fiscal space refers to the capacity of a government to provide resources for public priorities without compromising its ability to maintain fiscal stability and meet existing obligations.

A government may have a development priority but lack the budgetary flexibility to finance it. High debt-service costs, weak revenue collection, large recurrent expenditure commitments and economic shocks can limit the resources available for new investment.

This is particularly important for infrastructure because projects often require substantial upfront spending and deliver economic benefits over many years.

A government that cannot plan beyond its immediate budget cycle may struggle to develop the infrastructure required for long-term growth.

Fiscal space should therefore be understood not only as the ability to borrow, but also as the ability to raise revenue, manage expenditure, improve public investment efficiency and mobilise additional sources of finance.

The scale of Africa's infrastructure needs

The OECD's Africa's Development Dynamics 2025 estimates that Africa would need annual infrastructure investment of approximately USD 155 billion to reach the infrastructure levels associated with stronger productive transformation by 2040.

The estimate represents about 5.6% of Africa's GDP in 2024.

By comparison, average annual infrastructure investment was approximately USD 83 billion between 2016 and 2020, equivalent to around 3% of GDP.

The report also identifies a significant financing imbalance. Private investment represented approximately 11% of total infrastructure financing in the period examined.

These figures illustrate the scale of the challenge. Public budgets remain central to infrastructure development, but they cannot be expected to finance the entire investment requirement.

The question is how to improve the contribution of every source of capital while ensuring that projects are economically justified.

The pressure of debt servicing

Public debt is not inherently incompatible with development. Borrowing can be justified when it finances productive investments that generate economic and social returns over time.

The difficulty arises when the cost of servicing debt limits the government's ability to finance essential services and new infrastructure.

The OECD report found that African governments spent approximately seven times more on debt servicing than on infrastructure over the 2019–2023 period.

This comparison highlights the pressure that debt obligations can place on public investment.

It does not mean that all borrowing is unproductive or that infrastructure should always take priority over debt repayment. Governments must honour their obligations and preserve access to sustainable financing.

However, it does raise an important question about the structure and purpose of public borrowing.

Borrowing for infrastructure should be supported by credible feasibility assessments, realistic demand forecasts, transparent procurement and careful evaluation of long-term fiscal risks.

Projects should not be approved simply because financing is available.

Improving domestic revenue mobilisation

A sustainable infrastructure strategy requires stronger domestic revenue systems.

Tax revenue provides governments with resources to fund public investment without relying exclusively on borrowing or external assistance.

Improving revenue mobilisation does not necessarily mean increasing tax rates. It can involve broadening the tax base, improving compliance, reducing administrative inefficiencies, strengthening customs systems and addressing tax evasion.

Digital systems can support these efforts by improving data management, identifying compliance risks and making tax administration more efficient.

However, revenue policy must consider its effect on businesses, households and investment. Poorly designed taxation can weaken economic activity and reduce the very revenue governments are trying to mobilise.

The objective should be a tax system that is predictable, fair, administratively effective and capable of supporting long-term development.

Public investment efficiency

Increasing infrastructure expenditure is not enough if public investment is poorly selected or managed.

Governments need strong systems for identifying projects, conducting feasibility studies, assessing economic returns, managing procurement and monitoring implementation.

A road that connects productive agricultural areas to markets may generate wider economic benefits. A poorly planned road with limited demand may create maintenance costs without delivering comparable returns.

The same principle applies to energy, water, transport and digital infrastructure.

Public investment management should consider the entire project lifecycle, including construction, operation, maintenance and eventual replacement.

Maintenance is especially important. Neglecting existing infrastructure can create larger future costs and reduce the value of earlier public investment.

A credible infrastructure strategy must therefore balance new construction with the maintenance of existing assets.

The role of public-private partnerships

Public-private partnerships can help governments mobilise private capital and technical expertise for infrastructure development.

They can be useful where a project has identifiable revenue streams, measurable performance requirements and a structure that allocates risks to the parties best able to manage them.

However, a PPP does not make an infrastructure project free.

Governments may still provide guarantees, availability payments, land, subsidies or other forms of support. These commitments can create long-term fiscal obligations.

A project that appears affordable at the beginning may become expensive if demand forecasts are unrealistic, currency movements increase costs or contractual risks are poorly allocated.

PPP decisions should therefore be based on value-for-money assessments, transparent procurement and full disclosure of direct and contingent public liabilities.

Private participation should be treated as a financing and delivery option, not as a substitute for sound public investment planning.

Development finance and institutional capital

Development finance institutions can help bridge financing gaps by providing long-term loans, guarantees, technical assistance and risk-sharing instruments.

They can also support project preparation and help improve the quality of infrastructure investment opportunities presented to private investors.

Domestic pension funds, insurance companies and other institutional investors may also contribute to long-term infrastructure financing, provided that investment structures are appropriately regulated and risks are properly assessed.

The development of local capital markets is important because infrastructure projects often require long-term financing. Greater access to domestic-currency finance can also reduce exposure to foreign-exchange movements.

Nevertheless, institutional investors have obligations to their beneficiaries and policyholders. Infrastructure investments must meet appropriate standards of risk management, governance and financial performance.

A more disciplined approach to development finance

Africa's infrastructure ambitions require a shift from focusing only on how much governments spend to examining what that expenditure achieves.

Fiscal discipline and infrastructure investment should not be treated as opposing objectives. Strong public financial management can improve the ability of governments to finance development sustainably.

This requires better revenue mobilisation, more efficient expenditure, credible debt management, stronger project appraisal and transparent reporting of public liabilities.

It also requires regional cooperation. Cross-border transport, energy and digital infrastructure can create benefits that extend beyond individual national budgets.

The central issue is not whether Africa should invest more in infrastructure. It is how to ensure that additional investment produces lasting economic value.

The continent needs infrastructure that lowers production costs, connects businesses to markets, improves public services and supports productive economic activity.

Fiscal space should be created and managed with that objective in mind.
