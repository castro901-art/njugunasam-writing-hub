Culture Is the Missing Layer in AI

Why we're exploring culturally aware artificial intelligence at Trendify

I want to start with something small that bothers me.

Ask an AI a question about life in Kenya and you'll usually get a good answer. It's correct, neatly written, and sounds like it was written by someone who has read a lot about us but has never sat at our table.

It might explain why greetings are important in African communities, describe the role of elders or provide an interpretation of a traditional saying. But explaining something and understanding it are two different things.

It can tell you what a proverb means without understanding when you would actually use it. It can translate a greeting without capturing the respect, familiarity or relationship carried by the words. It can produce a grammatically correct sentence that no one in the community would naturally say.

That's the gap I keep running into. It's what we're working on at Trendify through CA-AALLM, our research into a Culturally Aware African Large Language Model.

I believe culture is one of the missing layers in artificial intelligence. And if AI is going to become part of everyday life in Africa, that layer cannot be an afterthought.

Correct isn't the same as familiar

When my people speak, a lot is happening beneath the words.

How you greet an elder matters. So does what you don't say, the relationship between the people speaking, the setting and the proverb that arrives at exactly the right moment.

Language is not simply a collection of words arranged into sentences. It carries history, relationships, humour, identity and social expectations.

A Kikuyu grandmother, a Maasai elder in Kajiado and a Somali trader in Eastleigh might ask the same question. They would still expect to be answered in different ways.

Not necessarily because the meaning of the question has changed, but because the cultural context has.

A phrase can communicate respect in one setting and sound distant in another. Humour can depend on shared experiences. A proverb may carry a meaning that cannot be understood through a literal translation. Even silence can communicate something.

Most AI systems have been developed using training resources in which major global languages and their associated cultural contexts are disproportionately represented. African languages and the knowledge systems surrounding them remain underrepresented in many of these resources.

The result is a familiar problem. The machine does what it can with what it has learned. Sometimes it translates too literally. Sometimes it produces a formal response where a natural one is expected. Sometimes it relies on generalisations that flatten differences between communities.

The problem isn't necessarily that the system cannot produce the language. It's that producing the language does not automatically mean understanding how it is used.

That distinction is central to our work.

I'm not tribeless, and I won't pretend to be

I've always said openly that I can't claim to be tribeless.

Where I come from shaped how I talk, what makes me laugh, how I greet people, and what I understand without anyone explaining it. That's true for everyone, whether they say it out loud or not.

I know "tribeless" usually comes from a good place. People mean they want unity. They want us to move beyond divisions that have caused so much harm.

I understand that intention. But I don't believe unity requires cultural erasure.

It shouldn't require someone to distance themselves from their language, traditions or community to demonstrate that they belong to a larger national or African identity.

We can be proud of where we come from and still respect where everyone else comes from. We can preserve our differences without turning them into barriers.

That belief sits at the heart of this work.

If culture matters to us as people, it has to matter to what we build. An AI that treats every community as one voice is quietly asking everyone to become interchangeable.

We're trying to build something different: systems that recognise cultural diversity rather than treating it as noise.

What we're building at Trendify

There are two names worth understanding here: CA-AALLM and TAM.

CA-AALLM stands for Culturally Aware African Large Language Model. It is the research initiative through which we're exploring how AI systems can better understand African languages, cultural context and community-specific knowledge.

TAM stands for Tiff Advanced Models. It is the model initiative emerging from this research.

The distinction matters. CA-AALLM describes the research direction; TAM describes the models we're working towards.

Our starting point is simple: AI should learn from communities, not just about them.

That requires more than collecting translations or assembling dictionaries. We need to understand how language operates in real situations, how meaning changes with context, and how knowledge is expressed within different communities.

We also need to recognise that African communities are not culturally interchangeable. A model should not assume that an expression, tradition or social expectation belonging to one community applies to another.

The ambition is not to create a single generic African personality for AI. It is to develop systems capable of recognising and responding to distinct cultural and linguistic contexts.

Starting with six communities

We began by identifying six communities whose languages and cultural contexts would form part of our initial research direction:

  • Kikuyu (Gĩkũyũ) - with particular attention to language structure, everyday expression, oral traditions, proverbs and the cultural context in which the language is used.
  • Maasai in Kenya - exploring Maa language and the cultural knowledge associated with its use in Kenyan communities.
  • Maasai in Tanzania - recognising that language and cultural practices should not automatically be treated as identical across national borders.
  • Nubian communities in Kenya - considering the linguistic and cultural experiences of Kenyan Nubian communities.
  • Ogiek - an important part of the research because of the relationship between language, community identity, heritage and indigenous knowledge.
  • Somali communities in Kenya - exploring the language and cultural contexts of Somali-speaking communities within Kenya.

This initial scope is intentional. We don't want to approach African culture as one enormous dataset that can be assembled without understanding the people represented in it.

Each community has its own history, language practices, expressions and knowledge systems. Those differences should remain visible throughout the research.

For each community, we've been developing documentation intended to capture aspects of how people live, communicate and express cultural knowledge. For Gĩkũyũ, we've also written a handbook to guide how the model learns and approaches the language.

These materials are an important starting point, not evidence that the cultural and linguistic challenges have already been solved.

There is more documentation to develop, more community engagement to undertake and more validation required.

Beyond translation: teaching AI context

One of the things I find most interesting about this work is the difference between translating a sentence and understanding what someone means.

Consider a simple greeting.

A translation system might identify the words, find their equivalents in another language and return a grammatically acceptable sentence.

But a culturally aware system should consider more.

Who is speaking? Who are they addressing? Is the conversation formal or informal? Is the speaker addressing an elder, a friend, a family member or someone they have just met? Does the expression communicate familiarity, respect, concern or something else?

These are not always questions that can be answered by a dictionary.

The same challenge appears with proverbs, idioms, humour, oral histories and expressions that depend on shared cultural knowledge.

Our research therefore needs to consider several interconnected areas:

Language structure and everyday speech. How words are formed, how sentences are constructed, how expressions are used and how formal language differs from everyday conversation.

Cultural knowledge. The context surrounding traditions, social relationships, oral histories, community practices and commonly understood expressions.

Proverbs and idiomatic expressions. How figurative language communicates meaning beyond the literal definition of individual words.

Code-switching and multilingual communication. Many Africans move between languages naturally during a conversation. A person might begin in Kiswahili, introduce an English technical term and finish with an expression from their mother tongue. AI should not automatically interpret this as an error.

Dialect and regional variation. A language is not necessarily spoken in precisely the same way everywhere. Differences in pronunciation, vocabulary and expression matter.

Tone and social context. The way something is communicated can be just as important as the words themselves.

We don't expect to solve all these challenges at once. But they need to be part of how we approach the problem from the beginning.

The community must be part of the intelligence

There is another issue I consider particularly important: who owns the knowledge being used to develop these systems?

African languages and cultural knowledge belong to communities with their own histories, rights and institutions. The development of AI should not become another process through which knowledge is extracted, commercialised and returned to the people it came from as a product they have no influence over.

If a system is being developed around a community's language, that community should have a meaningful voice in how its knowledge is documented, represented and used.

This is why community participation, informed consent and data governance need to be central to our approach.

We need to consider how language materials are collected, who has permission to contribute them, what information should remain private, and how communities can be represented accurately.

Some knowledge is appropriate for public education. Other knowledge may be restricted by cultural practices, community expectations or individual privacy. A responsible AI project must understand that not everything that can be collected should automatically become training material.

We also need to take data sovereignty seriously. African communities should not lose all meaningful influence over their linguistic and cultural resources simply because those resources are used in digital systems.

This is not just a technical consideration. It is an ethical and institutional responsibility.

Why this matters beyond language

I see this work having implications far beyond making an AI assistant more conversational.

Think about education.

A student should be able to interact with an AI system using a familiar language and receive explanations that recognise their linguistic context. This could support learning, particularly where students are more comfortable discussing certain concepts in their home languages.

Think about agriculture.

A farmer seeking information about crops, weather or livestock should not have to navigate an unfamiliar technical vocabulary before receiving useful assistance. Language and context can influence whether information is understood and applied.

Think about healthcare and public services.

People interact with institutions through language. When information is presented in ways that are unfamiliar or difficult to understand, access becomes harder. Culturally and linguistically aware systems could help make information more accessible, provided they are properly validated and used with appropriate safeguards.

Think about the preservation of languages.

Digital technology increasingly influences which languages are used in education, business, entertainment and communication. If a language remains absent from these systems, it risks becoming further marginalised in the digital environment.

AI cannot replace the people who speak, teach and preserve these languages. But it could become another tool supporting their continued use, documentation and transmission.

There is also an economic dimension.

Culturally aware AI creates opportunities for African technology companies to develop specialised products rather than simply adapting systems designed for other markets. It opens possibilities in education technology, enterprise software, public services, research and language technology.

For Africa, this is an opportunity to participate in the development of AI itself, not only its consumption.

The bigger question: who gets represented in the future of AI?

The global AI industry is being shaped by decisions about what data is collected, which languages receive attention, which markets attract investment and whose needs influence product development.

These decisions have consequences.

If African languages and cultural contexts are consistently underrepresented, the systems built around them may continue to serve African users through assumptions developed elsewhere.

I don't think that should be accepted as inevitable.

Africa has intellectual traditions, linguistic diversity and cultural knowledge that deserve serious consideration in the development of modern technology.

The continent should not have to choose between preserving its cultural identity and participating in technological progress.

We should be able to do both.

And that means African researchers, developers, universities, communities and businesses need to play a more active role in shaping the systems that will increasingly influence everyday life.

Where this goes

We're still early in this journey.

There is more listening to do, more documentation to develop, more testing with the people these languages belong to, and plenty of humility still to learn.

We need native speakers, linguists, cultural researchers, educators and community representatives to challenge our assumptions and help us understand where our approach is falling short.

We also need to be honest about the technical difficulty. Cultural awareness is not something that can be achieved simply by adding a few cultural facts to an AI model. It requires careful research, reliable language resources, meaningful community participation, evaluation and continuous improvement.

I would rather we take the time to build this properly than rush to announce something we have not yet achieved.

At Trendify, our ambition through CA-AALLM and TAM is to contribute to an AI ecosystem in which African languages and cultural knowledge are not treated as secondary considerations.

I want a future in which someone can interact with an intelligent system without feeling that they must leave their cultural identity at the door.

If you come from one of these communities, or you work with language and culture anywhere in Africa, I'd really like to hear from you.

Because the future of African AI should not be built by speaking about African communities without them.

It should be built with them.
