Artificial Intelligence and Africa's Economic Future

Productivity, public finance and the opportunity to build an African AI economy

“Development can be seen, it is argued here, as a process of expanding the real freedoms that people enjoy.”

— Amartya Sen, Development as Freedom (1999), p. 3

Artificial intelligence is becoming an important part of the global economy. It is changing how businesses operate, how governments deliver services, how financial institutions assess risk and how knowledge is produced. For African economies, the question is not simply whether AI will become widely adopted. It is whether the continent can develop the infrastructure, skills, institutions and investment required to participate meaningfully in this transformation.

Africa has an opportunity to approach AI as more than a consumer of imported technology. It can become a place where AI products are developed, adapted to local conditions, commercialised and deployed to solve problems across its economies.

That opportunity, however, will depend on economic decisions made today.

AI is an economic productivity question

The economic value of artificial intelligence lies partly in its ability to improve how existing resources are used.

In agriculture, AI-enabled tools can support crop monitoring, weather analysis, agricultural advisory services and supply-chain planning. In financial services, machine learning can help identify suspicious transactions, assess risk and improve operational efficiency. In healthcare, AI systems can support administrative processes, medical-image analysis and the organisation of health information. In manufacturing and logistics, predictive systems can improve maintenance, inventory management and resource allocation.

These applications do not automatically produce economic growth. Their value depends on whether businesses and public institutions can integrate them into real operations, whether workers have the necessary skills and whether the cost of adoption is justified by the productivity gains.

For African economies, this distinction matters. The priority should not be adopting AI for its own sake. It should be using AI to improve productivity in sectors that already contribute to employment, trade, public revenue and economic activity.

The infrastructure behind intelligence

AI is often presented as software, but its economic foundations are physical.

Data centres require reliable electricity, cooling systems, connectivity, specialised hardware and substantial capital investment. AI applications also depend on telecommunications infrastructure, secure data systems and access to computing resources.

These requirements create a challenge for countries where electricity reliability, broadband access and digital infrastructure remain uneven.

The International Monetary Fund's 2026 analysis of AI in Sub-Saharan Africa identifies infrastructure, human capital and institutional capacity as important constraints on adoption. It also highlights opportunities for AI to improve productivity and public-service delivery.

This means that AI policy cannot be separated from energy policy, telecommunications investment, education and industrial development.

A country that wants to develop a competitive AI sector must consider the cost and availability of electricity, the affordability of computing infrastructure, access to high-quality data and the ability of local firms to obtain the technical resources they need.

The public finance opportunity

Artificial intelligence also has implications for how governments raise revenue and spend public money.

Tax administrations can use analytical systems to identify unusual patterns, improve compliance and support risk-based audits. Customs authorities can use data analysis to identify potential misclassification and improve inspections. Public procurement systems can use analytical tools to detect unusual bidding patterns and strengthen oversight.

AI can also support planning in healthcare, education, transport and social protection.

However, public institutions must be careful about how these systems are procured and deployed. Automated decisions affecting citizens should be explainable, subject to appropriate human oversight and open to challenge. Governments must also protect personal data and ensure that public services do not exclude people who lack digital access.

The fiscal implications extend beyond operational savings. Governments will need to invest in digital infrastructure, cybersecurity, staff training, data governance and responsible procurement. These investments should be evaluated against measurable public benefits.

AI should therefore be treated as a public investment decision, not simply a technology purchase.

Building local AI companies

The development of an African AI economy requires more than access to foreign software.

Local companies need opportunities to develop products, build specialised models, provide AI infrastructure and serve industries that are often overlooked by global technology providers.

African markets have distinct languages, cultural contexts, regulatory environments and economic structures. These differences create opportunities for companies that understand local users and can build technology around their needs.

There is also a role for universities, research institutions, public agencies and private investors. Collaboration can improve access to datasets, technical expertise, computing resources and commercial opportunities.

Investment should not be limited to consumer-facing applications. Infrastructure, enterprise software, language technology, data services, cybersecurity and specialised AI systems are also important parts of the value chain.

The objective should be to create an environment in which African companies can move from experimenting with AI to developing commercially sustainable products.

Employment, skills and the changing economy

AI will change the nature of work, although the effects will differ across occupations and industries.

Some tasks will become automated. Others will be performed more efficiently with AI assistance. New roles will emerge in software development, data management, system integration, cybersecurity, product development and AI governance.

The policy response should focus on helping workers and businesses adapt. Education systems need to strengthen digital literacy, analytical thinking, technical skills and the ability to work with emerging technologies.

Governments and employers should also consider how existing workers can transition into roles where technology complements rather than replaces their capabilities.

For Africa, the opportunity is not merely to prepare workers for jobs created elsewhere. It is to develop the skills needed to build and operate technology within African economies.

A development strategy, not a technology race

Africa does not need to reproduce the exact AI development models of the United States, Europe or Asia. Its approach should reflect its own economic priorities.

That means supporting local research, strengthening electricity and connectivity, investing in education, improving public-sector digital capacity and creating predictable conditions for private investment.

It also means recognising that not every country needs to build every layer of AI infrastructure independently. Regional collaboration can help countries share research capacity, technical expertise, computing resources and standards.

The central economic challenge is to ensure that AI adoption contributes to productivity, employment, competitiveness and stronger public institutions.

Africa should not measure its progress only by the number of AI applications launched or the amount of technology imported. It should measure progress by the value created within its economies, the capabilities developed locally and the improvements delivered to businesses and citizens.

The future of AI in Africa will be shaped not only by technological advances, but by the quality of the economic decisions made around them.
