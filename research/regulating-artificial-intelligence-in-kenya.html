Regulating Artificial Intelligence in Kenya

Why protecting innovation must be part of protecting the public interest

“The only freedom which deserves the name, is that of pursuing our own good in our own way, so long as we do not attempt to deprive others of theirs, or impede their efforts to obtain it.”

— John Stuart Mill, On Liberty (1859), ch. I

Kenya wants to become a technology-driven economy. It wants to attract investment, develop local companies, create skilled employment and participate in the industries shaping the global economy.

Artificial intelligence is one of those industries.

But building an AI economy requires more than ambition. It requires an environment in which entrepreneurs can experiment, researchers can develop new products, investors can support promising companies and businesses can bring technology to market.

This is why the debate around Kenya's proposed Artificial Intelligence Bill, 2026, deserves careful attention.

The question is not whether artificial intelligence should be regulated. AI systems can create real risks involving privacy, discrimination, security, misinformation and accountability. Citizens deserve protection, and companies should be responsible for the systems they develop and deploy.

The question is whether regulation can achieve those objectives without making it unnecessarily difficult for Kenyan companies to participate in the AI economy.

My position is that Kenya should pursue responsible AI governance, but it should not create a framework that places disproportionate burdens on the startups it needs to develop.

The cost of entering the AI industry

Building an AI company is already expensive.

Startups may need to pay for computing infrastructure, cloud services, software development, research, data management, cybersecurity, skilled employees and product testing.

Unlike large multinational technology companies, early-stage businesses generally operate with limited capital and small teams. They may not have dedicated legal departments, compliance officers or large budgets for regulatory processes.

If regulation introduces extensive registration, assessment, documentation and compliance requirements without considering company size and actual risk, those requirements can become a barrier to entry.

This does not mean that small companies should be exempt from protecting users. It means that regulatory obligations should be proportionate to the risks created by the systems being developed or deployed.

A small company developing a low-risk productivity tool should not automatically face the same regulatory burden as an organisation deploying AI in critical infrastructure, healthcare or other high-impact settings.

The distinction is important.

A regulatory framework that treats every AI application in the same way risks discouraging experimentation and concentrating the ability to operate in the hands of companies with the greatest financial resources.

The danger of regulating before building capacity

Kenya is still developing the infrastructure and institutional capacity needed to participate more deeply in the global AI industry.

Local companies need access to affordable computing, reliable electricity, technical skills, investment and research opportunities.

If compliance becomes disproportionately expensive, some entrepreneurs may delay product development, reduce their ambitions or choose to establish operations in jurisdictions where the regulatory environment is more predictable.

These are risks that policymakers should evaluate rather than assume away.

The economic consequences could extend beyond individual startups. A weaker domestic AI sector could mean fewer skilled jobs, less local research, reduced private investment and greater dependence on imported technology.

Kenya should be careful not to create a situation in which the cost of entering the AI industry becomes an advantage reserved for the largest companies.

Regulation must be proportionate to risk

A sensible AI framework should distinguish between different uses of technology.

An application that helps a small business organise its records does not present the same risks as a system used to make consequential decisions about access to employment, financial services or public benefits.

Regulatory obligations should reflect factors such as the potential harm, the sensitivity of the data involved, the scale of deployment and the consequences of an error.

This approach would allow regulators to focus their attention and resources on systems that present the greatest risks.

It would also give smaller developers greater clarity about the obligations that apply to their products.

Proportionate regulation is not weaker regulation. It is regulation that directs oversight where it is most necessary.

The question of institutional duplication

Kenya already has institutions with responsibilities relevant to digital governance, privacy and data protection.

The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner, for example, plays an important role in enforcing the Data Protection Act.

Any new AI governance structure should be designed with these existing responsibilities in mind.

Creating additional institutions without clearly defining their relationship with existing regulators could increase administrative complexity for businesses and government agencies.

It could also create uncertainty about which authority is responsible for particular issues.

Kenya should establish clear institutional responsibilities, effective coordination and accessible guidance before adding layers of administration.

The objective should be stronger governance, not a larger regulatory structure for its own sake.

What Kenya should do instead

Kenya needs an AI policy that protects citizens while supporting the development of local technology companies.

A better framework would have several important features.

First, risk-based obligations. Requirements should reflect the actual risk posed by an AI system. High-impact applications should receive more intensive oversight than low-risk tools.

Second, proportional compliance. Smaller companies should have clear, achievable obligations. Regulatory requirements should consider company size, deployment scale and the nature of the technology.

Third, regulatory sandboxes. Startups and researchers should be able to test new applications under appropriate supervision. This would allow regulators to understand emerging technologies while giving developers a predictable environment for experimentation.

Fourth, institutional coordination. Existing regulators should have clearly defined responsibilities, with mechanisms for cooperation and the avoidance of unnecessary duplication.

Fifth, practical guidance. Developers need accessible standards, clear timelines and predictable procedures. Uncertainty itself can become a cost of doing business.

Finally, accountability. Companies should remain responsible for protecting personal data, preventing discrimination, addressing security risks and providing appropriate safeguards where AI systems affect people's rights and opportunities.

AI policy is also economic policy

The development of artificial intelligence has implications for investment, employment, public finance and national competitiveness.

Governments that create predictable environments for innovation can attract businesses, support research and encourage the development of new industries.

Governments that introduce complex and disproportionate requirements risk making investment less attractive, particularly for early-stage companies.

This does not mean regulation alone determines where investment flows. Infrastructure, talent, market size, access to capital and institutional stability also matter.

But regulatory design is one of the factors policymakers can influence.

Kenya should therefore evaluate AI legislation not only through the lens of legal compliance, but also through its potential effects on entrepreneurship, investment, research and technological development.

The choice Kenya faces

Kenya has the opportunity to build an AI industry that responds to local needs and contributes to the wider African economy.

That opportunity requires an environment where responsible companies can develop technology without facing unnecessary barriers.

Protecting citizens and supporting innovation are not competing objectives. Good regulation should achieve both.

Kenya should regulate harmful uses of AI, protect personal data, establish accountability and provide appropriate safeguards. But it should avoid imposing obligations that make it disproportionately difficult for local startups to compete.

The country should not make innovation an expensive privilege.

A successful AI policy will not be measured only by the number of rules created or institutions established. It will also be measured by whether Kenyan companies can grow, whether investment is encouraged, whether local talent can build valuable products and whether citizens benefit from the technology.

Kenya needs regulation that builds confidence in its AI ecosystem, not regulation that makes the ecosystem harder to build.
